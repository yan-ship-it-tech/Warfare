# How the War Changed Warfare: A Russian-Side Lessons-Learned Reference

*Synthesized from six primary sources (Aug 2024–May 2026 vintage). Compiled for a multi-audience learning platform: soldiers/small-unit leaders, instructors/training commands, policymakers, and defense-industry readers.*

---

## 1. Purpose and how to read this document

This reference cross-checks six documents against each other and pulls out the lessons that recur across independent sources — the strongest signal that something is a genuine, durable change in warfare rather than one unit's anecdote. It deliberately stays at the level of **doctrine, organization, technology trends, and defensive practice**. It does not reproduce step-by-step attack procedures, booby-trap/anti-handling mechanisms, mine-emplacement instructions, or chemical-agent employment details found in the source material — those are flagged only as threat categories personnel should recognize, not as how-to content.

## 3. Cross-cutting lessons

### 3.1 The drone layer now organizes everything else
Every source, regardless of audience or authorship, treats UAV presence as the dominant fact of the battlefield rather than one input among many:
- The National Guard manual (source 4) and MADI textbook (source 5) both restructure basic air-defense doctrine — originally built around manned aircraft — around small UAV detection and engagement instead.
- *Move, Shoot, Chat* devotes five of its 25 chapters directly to drones (evolution, countering, spotting, defensive positioning, pilot-specific tips) — more chapter space than weapons, first aid, or assault operations individually get.
- The Troika Compendium (source 7) independently confirms this from the U.S. Army side: Russian "Fires" and "Intelligence" functions have become inseparable from UAV reconnaissance-strike integration, to the point that the traditional warfighting-function boundaries in U.S. doctrine don't map cleanly onto how Russian units now operate.

**Implication:** any platform or curriculum still treating "drones" as a subtopic under ISR or fires is organized around a battlefield that no longer exists. The sources converge on drones as connective tissue across every warfighting function.

### 3.2 Command is decentralizing under drone pressure — but unevenly
Source 7 states this explicitly and technically: units are being forced into smaller teams, faster low-level decision-making, and more flexible logistics because massed formations are now visible and targetable almost immediately. Source 1/2 (Markin) corroborates from the ground level — assault groups have shrunk to 3–5 personnel, and "strongpoints" have become dispersed two-man positions.

Where the sources disagree is on how well this decentralization is actually working. Source 7 flags that Russian command culture is still "highly centralized and rigid" by design (a matter of doctrine, not adaptation failure), creating friction with the small-unit independence that survival now requires. Markin's interviewees complain about the same tension from inside: bypassed chains of command, units hoarding drones instead of pooling reconnaissance, and incentive structures that reward video-friendly kills over units actually holding ground.

**Implication for instructors/training commands:** the technology has changed faster than the command culture built to employ it. Training that teaches drone tactics without addressing delegation of authority to the team level is solving half the problem.

### 3.3 Sustainment and protection are converging
Source 7's "Sustainment" chapter and Markin's reports independently describe the same shift: robotic ground platforms and "heavy" drones are increasingly used for resupply and casualty evacuation specifically *because* manned logistics runs are now one of the highest-casualty activities on the battlefield. This is a genuinely new coupling — logistics risk is now largely a subset of counter-UAV risk, not a separate problem.

### 3.4 The EW/counter-EW cycle has produced physical control lines, not just jamming
Fiber-optic-controlled FPV drones (immune to RF jamming) appear independently in the FPV tactics guide (source 3), the MADI curriculum (source 5, listed as a "promising area"), and Markin's reports. This is a case where a single technical fix (a physical control line instead of radio) has forced a doctrinal response across multiple institutions simultaneously — a useful marker of which adaptations are structural versus which are still improvised.

### 3.5 Training gaps identified by the sources themselves
Two recurring, source-acknowledged gaps:
- **Holding vs. assaulting.** Markin's interviewees report heavy assault training but thin training in digging in and holding ground — and link this directly to losses in the "holding" phase.
- **EW equipment literacy.** *Move, Shoot, Chat* and the National Guard manual both note that units often possess electronic countermeasure equipment without the trained personnel to maintain or employ it correctly, which the National Guard manual solves institutionally by designating non-staff UAV-countermeasure crews with dedicated training time — a model worth evaluating for adoption elsewhere.

### 3.6 Multi-domain implications
None of these sources treat "domains" as cleanly separable. Cheap UAVs now perform reconnaissance, precision strike, electronic warfare (via detection/jamming payloads), logistics, information/psychological operations (loudspeaker and leafleting drones), and even air-to-air intercept roles — one platform type crossing five traditional domain lines. The National Guard manual's air-defense doctrine and the MADI curriculum's "destruction of the enemy using UAVs" chapter both assume this convergence rather than argue for it, which is itself the signal: institutionally, this is no longer treated as novel.

## 4. Implications by audience

**Soldiers / small-unit leaders**
- Camouflage, movement, and light discipline (Ch. 1–4, *Move, Shoot, Chat*; countermeasures section, source 3) are now survival-critical skills, not secondary ones — multiple sources put drone detection ahead of small-arms proficiency in casualty-avoidance value.
- The "holding ground" training gap above is a personal risk factor worth flagging in any curriculum aimed at this audience.
- Awareness (not handling) of booby-trapped/tampered ordnance and unfamiliar drone wreckage is repeatedly emphasized — the consistent instruction across sources is *don't approach, mark the location, report it, wait for a specialist.*

**Instructors / training commands**
- The command-decentralization gap (3.2) and the holding/assault training imbalance (3.5) are the two most actionable, source-corroborated curriculum fixes.
- The National Guard's non-staff UAV-countermeasure crew model (source 4) is a concrete organizational template — dedicated, trained, on-call teams rather than ad hoc individual responses.

**Policymakers**
- Production-scale figures across sources (e.g., tens of thousands of FPV units per month cited in industry reporting around this corpus) indicate that procurement and industrial-base questions are now inseparable from tactical questions — a battlefield technology with a consumer-electronics supply chain changes acquisition timelines in ways traditional defense procurement cycles aren't built for.
- Source 7's framing (a U.S. institutional read of Russian adaptation) is the most useful document for calibrating how much of this is genuine Russian-military change versus how much is contested/still-evolving — worth prioritizing for policy audiences over the practitioner volumes.

**Defense companies**
- Fiber-optic control, computer-assisted homing, and delivery-drone applications are all flagged as "promising areas" in the MADI curriculum (source 5) — i.e., areas the Russian military-education system is actively building workforce capacity around, which is a leading indicator of procurement direction.
- Counter-UAV remains fragmented and under-resourced at the unit level relative to the offensive drone ecosystem across every source — the clearest capability gap, and the one most consistently self-reported by the practitioner sources.

## 5. What this document deliberately leaves out

Consistent with the scope agreed for this platform: anti-handling/booby-trap mechanisms for downed drones, mine-emplacement procedures and model-specific targeting details, and chemical-agent application methods appear in the source material but are not reproduced here. Where relevant, personnel-facing sections above note these as recognize-and-report threat categories rather than technical content.
