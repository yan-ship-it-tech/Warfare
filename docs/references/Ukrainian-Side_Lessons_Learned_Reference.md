# How the War Changed Warfare: A Ukrainian-Side Lessons-Learned Reference

*Companion volume to the Russian-Side and Western-Side Lessons-Learned References. Synthesized from Ukraine's own institutional doctrine, defense-tech ecosystem, military-academic literature, and senior officers' on-the-record analysis, cross-checked against the SSO Kursk-operation / AFU UAV-crew practitioner synthesis already built for this platform. Compiled for the same multi-domain warfare learning platform: soldiers/small-unit leaders, instructors/training commands, policymakers, and defense-industry readers.*

---

## 1. Purpose and how to read this document

This applies the same method as its companion volumes: cross-check independent sources against each other, and treat what recurs — not what one source claims about itself — as the signal of a genuine lesson. It replaces two earlier passes: a first draft that was a source catalog rather than a synthesis, and a second draft that synthesized but organized itself around Ukraine's *institutional process for learning* rather than around what Ukraine actually learned about fighting — a different, less platform-relevant question. This version is built around the same topic spine as the companion documents (drone-driven force design, C2 survivability, sustainment, the EW arms race, self-acknowledged gaps, and multi-domain convergence) so the three documents read as one comparable set.

It also draws on, but is structurally separate from, the practitioner-level synthesis already built for this platform from the SSO Kursk-operation compilation and the Central Research Institute AFU's *Reference for an Unmanned (System) Crew*. That document's seven independently-confirmed cross-source convergences are carried forward here as load-bearing content rather than re-derived.

As with both companion documents, this stays at the level of **doctrine, organization, technology trends, and defensive/protective practice**, and excludes step-by-step attack procedures, exact frequencies, and anti-handling/booby-trap mechanisms even where the underlying sources contain them. It also excludes diplomatic and alliance-political commentary — Zaluzhnyi's and Syrskyi's remarks on NATO membership prospects or US policy sit outside this platform's scope regardless of which side of the war they concern, matching how the Western-side document excludes equivalent NATO-side material.

## 3. Cross-cutting lessons

### 3.1 The drone layer reorganizes Ukrainian force design too — and institutions are still catching up to a fight that already changed
This is the same finding both companion documents lead with, and the Ukrainian-side sources reach it from their own direction. The AFU UAV-crew manual (source 2) documents 5–12 devices routinely expended per target as *standard* doctrine, not a failure mode, and SSO after-action material (source 1) describes multi-sortie engagements with mid-engagement camouflage adjustment against a single target — drone employment as a saturating, iterative process, not one-shot precision. Institutionally, Ukraine registered this by standing up the Unmanned Systems Forces as an independent branch in February 2024 and an Unmanned Air Defense Forces element inside the Air Force. But Ukraine's own peer-reviewed technical journals (source 5) were still publishing foundational-level papers on swarm-UAV C2 effectiveness and Class-1 UAS tactical employment as recently as 2024–2026 — the doctrine and the science are still being written *after* units had already been fighting this way for two-plus years. **The same lag the Western-side document names for NATO (its 3.1) shows up inside Ukraine's own institutions, just running faster.**

### 3.2 Command posts and small C2 elements survive on dispersal and architecture, not distance
Carried forward from sources 1–2, confirmed independently by both: a small HQ or a single UAV crew's C2 setup that works at small scale breaks down as scope grows. The AFU's UAV C2 software addresses this by splitting commander, operator, and administrator functions across separate workstations feeding a shared field server, rather than concentrating everything on one operator. PACE comms planning explicitly ranks assets by reliability under contact rather than theoretical bandwidth — tactical radio → relay → satellite directional → satellite omnidirectional, with the highest-bandwidth option ranked *last* on purpose — and deliberate physical separation of C2 elements recurs at every scale, from a full TOC/logistics split down to a single UAV crew's launch/operator/landing-point separation. This is the same conclusion the Western-side document reaches from the CALL/TDF manual (its 3.2, "detectability, not distance"): independently arrived at from a different Ukrainian source line.

### 3.3 Sustainment decentralizes under the same pressure as command, and reset time is the quiet killer
Carried forward from sources 1–2: units systematically underestimate the time needed to reset between missions — refuel, rearm, re-establish comms — and the documented fix is pre-staging fuel, ammunition, and comms equipment at the home base rather than simply "planning tighter." This parallels the Russian-side document's finding (its 3.3) that robotic ground platforms and resupply drones are absorbing logistics risk specifically because manned logistics runs are now among the highest-casualty activities — the same structural pressure, addressed on the Ukrainian side through pre-staging discipline rather than robotic substitution as the first-line fix.

### 3.4 The EW arms race produced the same physical-control-line answer on both sides, and Ukraine is also trying a second approach
Russia fielded fiber-optic-controlled FPV drones first, in the Kursk region from around August 2024, specifically to defeat Ukrainian RF jamming. Ukraine's own Ministry of Defence began certifying domestic fiber-optic models within months — Brave1 ran its first fiber-optic test flights in late March 2025, and the Ministry had certified roughly 40 models by that April. This is a rare case where the timeline itself is the lesson: **a single technical fix, adopted by one side under pressure, was replicated by the other side inside about six months**, which is a useful marker for how fast a genuinely effective adaptation propagates across a contested front, regardless of who invents it. Ukraine has also been pursuing a second, distinct route to the same jamming-immunity goal — FPV drones guided by onboard machine vision rather than a physical tether — described in industry reporting as immature but active, alongside fiber optic rather than as a replacement for it. Underneath both approaches, the AFU UAV-crew manual (source 2) treats analog/mechanical fallback — dual GPS/GLONASS cross-checking, mandatory compass/sun/star navigation skill, a scripted lost-signal algorithm — as the *default* design baseline, not a contingency: Ukrainian doctrine assumes the primary electromagnetic system will be denied.

### 3.5 Ukraine's own sources say counter-UAS is still an integration problem, not a hardware one — and Western analysis independently agrees
Ukrainian peer-reviewed military-technical journals (source 5) are still treating swarm-UAV C2 effectiveness and coordinated counter-drone response as open research questions rather than settled doctrine. This matches the Western-side document's finding (its 3.1, citing a Modern War Institute analysis) that Ukraine's own counter-drone effort "lacks a single point of mission accountability and is fragmented across services." **An independent Western institutional read and Ukraine's own academic press arrive at the same gap from two different directions** — this is one of the stronger three-document convergences in the whole corpus, because none of the three sources involved were checking the others' work.

### 3.6 Procurement has become a doctrine-speed feedback loop — Ukraine's most distinctive institutional adaptation
Brave1's e-Points/marketplace system (source 4) converts battlefield performance directly into equipment allocation within weeks: units earn points for verified kills and re-spend them on whichever certified system is currently working, and manufacturers see unit-level performance data directly. This is functionally a lessons-learned process that skips the written report entirely and acts on the data. It's Ukraine's own answer to a gap named from the outside in both companion documents — the Russian-side document notes that production-scale questions are now inseparable from tactical ones, and the Western-side document finds NATO's 2026 exercise cycle still oriented at integrating existing systems rather than restructuring procurement around live battlefield data (its 3.1). *Caveat:* the specific figures (400+ units with direct ordering access, 500+ grants issued) are self-reported by Brave1 and the ministries involved, not independently audited — the mechanism is the well-supported claim here, not the precise numbers.

### 3.7 Multi-domain convergence and continuous force-structure revision are now permanent features, not transitional ones
Since February 2024, Ukraine has stood up the Unmanned Systems Forces, an Unmanned Air Defense Forces element, a distinct Assault Troops branch (created, per Syrskyi's own account, specifically because Assault Troops' rapid-response no-fixed-line role didn't fit inside Air Assault Troops' fixed-defense-line doctrine), and a cyber force — four structural changes in about two years. Zaluzhnyi's argument (source 7, Apr 2025) that doctrine needs continuous revision rather than periodic replacement, and his description of naval drones displacing surface-fleet functions, both describe the same pattern. This matches the Russian-side document's 3.6 and the Western-side document's 3.7: all three source bases now treat multi-domain integration as a description of how the war is already being fought, not a future-force goal any of them is still arguing toward — and on the Ukrainian side specifically, the force-structure churn shows this isn't settling into a fixed new shape either.

## 4. Implications by audience

**Soldiers / small-unit leaders**
- Analog/mechanical fallback (3.4) and PACE comms discipline (3.2) are core skills, not backup trivia — Ukrainian doctrine builds for jamming as the default case.
- Pre-staging fuel/ammo/comms before reset (3.3) is a documented fix for a documented, recurring underestimation — worth drilling as its own skill, not folded into general logistics training.
- Never handle a downed or unfamiliar drone — identical instruction to both companion documents.

**Instructors / training commands**
- The convergence set in §3.2, 3.3, and 3.4 — an elite SOF field-experience compilation and a formal AFU technical-institute manual independently landing on the same lessons — is close to a ready-made curriculum core.
- OP 7-162's structured-AAR requirement (source 6) is worth noting for methodology: it standardizes an after-action format both source 1 and source 2 were already using independently before the doctrine existed, which is itself a teaching point about how fast bottom-up practice can outpace top-down doctrine.
- 3.7's force-structure churn argues for teaching the *decision process* for when a branch or role split is warranted, not just the current org chart.

**Policymakers**
- 3.6's procurement-as-doctrine model is the most exportable, least-replicated Ukrainian institutional innovation in this corpus — worth weighing directly against the Western-side document's finding that NATO's own 2026 exercise cycle is still oriented at system integration rather than data-driven procurement.
- 3.5's three-way convergence on counter-UAS as an integration problem, not a hardware problem, is as solid a policy-relevant finding as this corpus produces anywhere.
- 3.4's six-month replication timeline for fiber-optic control is a concrete data point for how fast a proven adaptation crosses a contested front — a planning factor for how much lead time any capability advantage can be expected to hold.

**Defense companies**
- Brave1's e-Points/marketplace model (3.6) rewards demonstrated battlefield effect over specification sheets; vendors optimizing for that market behave differently than vendors optimizing for a traditional RFP process.
- The literature gap in 3.5 (swarm C2, Class-1 UAS tactics still described as open problems in Ukraine's own technical press) marks continued demand for outside contribution, not saturation.
- Machine-vision-guided FPV (3.4) is explicitly described in the record as immature but active — a second EW-resistance approach still open to outside development, distinct from the now-crowded fiber-optic space.

## 5. What this document deliberately leaves out

Consistent with both companion documents' scope: no step-by-step ambush/assault procedures, exact EW frequencies, or anti-handling/booby-trap mechanisms from the underlying SSO and UAV-crew source material, even where those sources contain this detail — flagged only as recognize-and-report threat categories. Zaluzhnyi's and Syrskyi's diplomatic and alliance-political remarks are excluded as out of scope regardless of source, matching how the Western-side document treats equivalent NATO-side material. Vendor names, firmware versions, and specific hardware line-ups from the AFU UAV-crew manual remain a dated snapshot, not durable content.
